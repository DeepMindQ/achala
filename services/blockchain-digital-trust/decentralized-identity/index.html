Privacy and governance overlay

Selective disclosure, beacon protection and the DPA file

Selective disclosure is structural, holders share claims, not documents, and the issuer charter caps what may be requested, so the ecystem minimizes data by design rather than by policy memo.

  • Governed issuance

    Only registry-listed issuers can mint

  • Status-in-path

  • Selective disclosure

  • Consent logging

    Holder consent is recorded and auditable

Revocation is tested like a control: synthetic cancelled credentials are presented to verifiers weekly, and any acceptance is a sev-1, revocation fiction is the ecosystem's most dangerous failure.

Adoption is a cold-start problem, not a cryptography problem

Why the trust triangle lives or dies on issuer economics and verifier convenience

Verifiable-credential ecosystems fail for the reason marketplaces fail: issuers will not issue without verifiers, verifiers will not verify without holders, and holders delete wallets that sit empty. The engineering answer is sequencing, not evangelism, pick the pair with existing economic gravity (an employer issuing to employees who already verify at a gate, a university issuing to students who already verify at a licensure board), ship the verifier SDK to the one-scan standard (a verification must be one action, under five seconds, with the trust registry doing the policy work), and let revocation propagation under ten minutes make the credentials operationally real rather than ceremonial.

Privacy review is the launch gate: selective disclosure, no beaconing to issuers (verifier queries must not leak to the issuer who issued), and retention rules for the status registry that survive DPA scrutiny. The identity console reads adoption honestly, issuance counts, verification counts, wallet retention, because a trust framework that is not being verified is a pilot, no matter how elegant the cryptography.

  • Sequence the triangle

    Start with the issuer-verifier pair that already has economic gravity.

  • One-scan verifier standard

    One action, under five seconds; the registry carries the policy.

  • Issuer-blind verification

    Verifier queries never leak back to issuers, privacy by protocol.

  • Fast revocation

    Status propagation under ten minutes makes credentials operationally real.

  • Why will people actually keep and use the wallet?

    The wallet ships inside apps people already use, the employee badge rides the workplace app, the membership proof rides the loyalty app. Adoption is a cold-start problem solved at the consumer-app intersection, not a new app to download.

  • How does verification work offline?

    Credentials carry cryptographic predicates that verify locally: 70–100% of the listed flows verify without a network call, and the console reports the offline-capable share per credential type as a signed design target.

  • What prevents issuer lock-in?

    The trust charter governs issuers, and credentials are standard verifiable credentials, a holder’s wallet is portable across issuers, and the charter amendment path is documented for exactly that reason.

  • How are revoked or expired credentials handled?

    Revocation registries are checked at verification wherever the network allows, and predicates carry expiry. The console tracks revocation latency per issuer, so a revoked credential stops verifying on evidence, not on schedule.

  • What does the trust-framework team inherit?

    The charter, the issuer onboarding kit, the verifier gates, the console and the privacy overlay, the ecosystem artifacts, not just code, because the charter is the load-bearing document.
