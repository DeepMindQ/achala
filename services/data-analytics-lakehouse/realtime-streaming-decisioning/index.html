Real-Time Streaming & Event-Driven Decisioning

Real-time is a budget — not a marketing adjective#

Hop accounting, provable semantics and the replay harness

Streaming decisioning is where data stops being a report and becomes a reflex: fraud holds in milliseconds, personalization on the next request, pricing that adapts mid-session. The engineering is stateful stream processing with honest semantics — exactly-once state, replayable events, and latency budgets per hop that are measured in production, not hoped for in the design doc.

The decision path is held to the same gate discipline as everything here: a policy change re-runs the replay suite before it touches live traffic, because a bad decision rule at 50k events per second is an incident, not a tweak.

Below: the failure modes that make streaming paths lie, the reference architecture that budgets latency honestly, and the console that shows decision health under real load.

Four ways streaming paths lie about themselves#

Vibe latency, silent drops, unversioned decisions, unproven replay

Every one of these produces a demo that works and a production incident that surprises nobody who measured — the countermeasures make the path's own behavior observable and bounded.

The decision path and its budget: six hops, 150ms#

Bus, state, rules and actions under one budget

The budget is designed before the code: hop-by-hop allocations, exactly-once state with checkpointed replay, and shadow lanes that compare decisions against the previous engine — disagreement is measured, not assumed away.

One event, 150 milliseconds, six hops#

The hop-by-hop accounting of a governed decision

From ingress through state lookup, scoring and the action write — with the late-event handling, out-of-order tolerance and replay path marked where they actually trigger.

The audit trail a review will actually read

Real-time is a budget with six hops#

Latency accounting, exactly-once discipline and the replay harness that proves parity

Real-time programs fail when latency is a vibe: the path claims milliseconds and the decision lands late. The engineering treats the path as a budget — end-to-end milliseconds allocated across the hops (ingest, bus, state, rules, action), each enforced with p99 checks, and the budget signed per decision class because fraud decisioning and inventory refresh do not share a tolerance. Exactly-once semantics are provable: event-sourced decisions in a durable state store, idempotent actions, and the audit trail that reconstructs any decision from its events.

The replay harness is the truth mechanism: decisions re-driven from the event log must produce parity with what ran — rule changes, model swaps and incidents are all validated by replay before production trusts them again. The console reads p99 per hop, budget adherence and replay parity; the incident review reads the decision audit, because a streaming decision that cannot explain itself is a liability at market speed.

  • Signed latency budgets

    Milliseconds per hop, per decision class — enforced with p99 checks.

  • Provable exactly-once

    Event-sourced state and idempotent actions; audit reconstructs decisions.

  • Replay parity harness

    Re-driven decisions must match what ran — before trust resumes.

  • Versioned decisions

    Rules and models side-by-side in the path, pinned and rolled back.

  • What does "real-time is a budget" mean?

    A number per hop: 150ms allocated across consume, validate, state update, enrich, decide and act. Measured p99 must hold headroom on each hop — a path without a budget is marketing, and the console shows where the money goes.

  • How do you guarantee exactly-once decisions?

    Idempotent state updates and transactional sinks: a replayed event produces the same decision once, and the gates require replay proof before live decisions. Duplicate actions are a correctness failure, not a volume footnote.

  • How are decisions made auditable?

    Every decision carries its inputs, the policy version and the state snapshot it saw — incident review reconstructs the decision path hop by hop, which the governance overlay treats as a first-class artifact.

  • What happens when a hop exceeds its budget?

    The budget degrades gracefully: degrade paths — skip enrichment, cached decisions — are pre-built and rehearsed, and sustained breach is a gate metric. Real-time that quietly becomes batch-at-scale is the failure the budget prevents.

  • Can this overlay our existing streaming stack?

    Yes — the integration surface names your streams, state stores and action systems; budgets overlay the existing topology, and the defaults table names where a hop earns a deviation.
