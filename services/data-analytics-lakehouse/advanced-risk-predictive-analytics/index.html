Advanced Risk & Predictive Analytics is delivered by an Achala practice team that stays through industrialization. Achala builds lakehouses with data contracts: producers and consumers agree on schema and quality, breaks are caught at the boundary, and every table carries an owner and a freshness target. Decisions downstream become defensible. Who this is for: CDOs and finance leaders who need numbers both sides sign, and teams tired of disputed data.

The practice spans credit and operational risk, demand and capacity forecasting, and early-warning systems, always with the governance layer that says who may act on the model and under what bounds.

A forecast earns trust by being falsifiable

Point-in-time discipline, walk-forward evals and the naive challenger

Risk analytics loses trust through leakage: features that quietly encode the future make backtests brilliant and production embarrassing. The engineering answer is point-in-time correctness as a property of the feature store, every feature carries its as-of semantics, joins are enforced through the point-in-time layer, and leakage tests (target-aware, run in CI) block any feature set that peeks. Evaluation is walk-forward: rolling-origin splits that respect time, holdouts the model has structurally never seen, and a baseline comparison where a naive seasonal benchmark is a formal challenger, because a model that cannot beat naive does not ship, whatever its AUC.

The accountability loop closes the trust: every forecast carries an owner, the metric it moves and a review date; outcomes are scored against the forecast with the deltas published on the console; and model-risk governance (documentation, validation, change control) is generated as a by-product of the pipeline rather than written after the fact. Falsifiability is the product: a forecast you can be wrong about publicly is a forecast the planning meeting can use.

  • Point-in-time by construction

    As-of semantics enforced in the store; joins cannot peek.

  • Leakage tests in CI

    Target-aware scans block peeking feature sets at build time.

  • Naive as formal challenger

    Seasonal baselines are scored too; the model must beat them.

  • Outcomes published

    Forecast-versus-actual deltas trended per use case, owner named.

  • How do you prove a forecast is actually better?

    Against a signed baseline: the seasonal-naive model a planner would otherwise use is the floor, and skill is measured on holdout windows horizon by horizon. A model that cannot beat the floor does not get a mandate.

  • What happens when the forecast is wrong?

    It is falsifiable by design: every forecast carries confidence bands and the decision it feeds, so a miss is diagnosable, feature drift, regime change, or a broken assumption, and the miss log feeds the retrain calendar.

  • Can you work with our existing planning systems?

    Yes, forecasts land in the planning systems through typed integration, and the action loop records which plan took which number, so accountability survives the handoff from model to decision.

  • How is model risk governed?

    The overlay carries inventory, validation evidence, drift monitoring and the retrain cadence, mapped to model-risk expectations so the examination pack is a byproduct of operation, not an annual scramble.

  • What data do you need to start?

    Enough history to sign a baseline and holdouts. The first two weeks prove join quality and horizon coverage; where history is short, the gates say so honestly instead of lending the forecast false confidence.
